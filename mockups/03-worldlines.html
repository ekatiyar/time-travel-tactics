Worldlines on the board

Today history is a cluster of 6px corner dots per tile: you can see that something was here, not who went where. This direction draws each player's recorded path as one continuous ribbon through tile centres, ordered by personal index, so a worldline reads as a route with a direction, a key riding on it, and a front cutting it.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Order is visible. A ribbon is read in index order, so you can see that Coral reached t12 before turning around, which a dot cluster cannot show.
  • Inversions get one glyph instead of a stack. Teal's four bodies at (14,1) t9 become one loop and a count.
  • The key becomes a thing that travels. A bead on indices 14 to 17 shows custody as a stretch of path, matching how fronts actually eat it.
  • The front reads as a cut at a place, not a number in a side panel. The notch sits on the exact body it will hit.
  • It answers "how did that body get there" without a click, which helps touch users who have no hover.

Cons

  • Four full ribbons on a 16×9 board is a lot of ink. The all-four view above is already near the limit; a longer match makes it worse.
  • Ribbons cross tiles they never occupied at the focus turn, so a line over a tile can be misread as presence there.
  • Retraced edges need offset lanes, and offset lanes drift off tile centres. At 18px cells the lanes merge.
  • The ribbon mixes two clocks. It is ordered by personal index but drawn in space, so a long ribbon segment can be one world turn or none at all, as at an inversion.
  • Onion rings and ribbons overlap on busy tiles. Tile (4,4) carries Coral 6 and 8, Amber 7 and 8, plus two ribbons.

What it answers

It serves one combined board for present and history directly: the focus turn stays the bright layer and everything else is path, not a second board. It serves identity and personal index on every body partly, because the ribbon carries identity continuously while the index still needs a label or hover. It serves the personal-index gap for an inbound front well: the notch on Coral index 14 with "4" puts the gap on the body instead of in a list.

It gives a real answer to how touch users inspect a history body, since the common question, where did this come from, is answered without any inspection at all. It leaves open erasure and restoration fronts, which are two travelling cuts plus a hole between them and would need a second ribbon treatment, and it does nothing for the personal-index colour ramp question, which page 17 takes up.

Cost

M. The data is already there. view.bodies carries colour, index, world turn and direction, and view.keys and view.fronts carry the rest, so nothing changes in the engine view. The work is all in the board renderer in ui.tsx: sort per colour by index, build a segment list, compute the offset lanes for retraced edges, and emit paths instead of the current dot clusters. Roughly 150 lines replacing the trail block, plus the loop and bead glyphs.

The real cost is the "mine only" default and its toggle, because the all-four view is not usable on a small board. Add one control beside the world-turn and look-back sliders, and the styling in style.css for ribbon opacity and dash. No engine change.
