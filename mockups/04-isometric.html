Isometric time stack

The flat board puts present and history on one plane and asks colour and opacity to carry the difference. This direction spends a spatial axis instead: tiles become diamonds, walls become raised blocks, and each earlier world turn is a translucent slab hanging below the focus layer, so history reads as depth rather than as dimness.

Four tiles, both ways

Tiles (3,3) to (4,4) at focus t8, look-back 3, at the same tile width in both. This is where Coral and Amber cross: Coral 5 to 8 walks through and Amber 6 to 10 inverts on top of it.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Depth is a free channel. Colour keeps meaning identity, fill keeps meaning direction, and age stops competing with either.
  • Same-tile stacks stop being stacks. Coral 12 and 13 at t12, or Amber 7 and 8 at t7, become two pawns on two slabs instead of a "12·13" label.
  • The worldline becomes a physical descent, so "which turn was that" has a spatial answer rather than an opacity you have to judge.
  • Walls read as walls. A raised block says "you cannot walk here" without relying on a fill value.
  • It gives the look-back slider something to do that you can see: each notch adds a visible plate.

Cons

  • Occlusion is severe and it is the central problem. The focus slab covers about 70% of the slab under it, so the default view hides most of what it claims to show.
  • The axis is world time, and half the fixture's interest is at t9 and above. An inverted player's recent history runs off the top of a stack that only goes down.
  • Tiles are no longer square, so grid reading gets harder: "two tiles left" is a diagonal, and reading (x,y) off a diamond takes practice.
  • Touch targets shrink. A past-slab pawn is about 26×20px, well under the 44px minimum, and the peel gesture competes with pan.
  • Cost: 144 diamonds and up to 15 wall blocks per slab, plus pawns. At four slabs that is roughly 700 SVG nodes against 144 rects today, and every look-back change rebuilds all of them.
  • Everything moves when the focus turn changes, so the whole stack re-renders each turn rather than a few chips.

What it answers

It answers one combined board for present and history literally, and answers identity and personal index on every body better than the flat board does, because every body gets its own diamond and its own label rather than sharing a tile. It gives a real handle on touch inspection of a history body: peel a slab, tap the pawn. That is a better gesture than a long press on a 5px dot.

It does not help with fronts. A front travels through personal index, which cuts across slabs diagonally and has no natural depth reading, so fronts still need the flat board's triangles or page 03's notch. It does not help with beyond-horizon turns either: those are above the focus, and this stack only goes down. And it actively argues against itself on should a player see their own known future, since an inverted player's whole recorded life is in the direction the stack does not render.

The honest recommendation is to treat this as an inspection mode rather than the main board. As a mode reached from a body, "show me the last four turns of this corner", the occlusion cost is paid on a 4×4 crop where it is cheap. As the default 16×9 view it hides more than it reveals.

Cost

L. This is a second renderer, not a change to the existing one. The board in ui.tsx is DOM rects on a CSS grid; this needs an SVG projection, a depth sort by x+y, wall block geometry, pawn geometry and slab transforms. Call it 400 lines of new rendering plus hit-testing, because a diamond is not a rect and pointer events need the painter order reversed.

No engine view change: view.bodies already carries the world turn per body, which is all a slab needs. The look-back value becomes the slab count directly. The parts that do need engine thought are the ones this view cannot draw, fronts and the horizon, and those stay on the flat board.

If it ships as an inspection mode on a small crop, it drops to M: the same projection over a 4×4 window, no peel gesture on desktop hover, and no need to solve hit-testing on 700 nodes.
