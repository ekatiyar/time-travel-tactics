Split presents

After an inversion the players stop sharing a now. Rook is at world turn 8, Juno and Ash are at 7, Mira is at 2. The board draws one world turn at a time and says nothing about whose present that turn is, so it is easy to plan a move against a body whose player left four turns ago. This page adds a lens: a chip per playhead, a strip that shows all four at once, and a forecast of who can meet whom.

Five of six pairs never meet, and the table shows why in one number. Two playheads walking toward each other close by two turns a meta-turn, so they land on the same turn only when the gap between them is even. Yours to Juno is 1. You pass.

The only thing that changes parity is an action that does not spend a world turn: an inversion, or being stuck. If Juno inverts on meta-turn 17 it stays at t7 while you arrive there, and you are suddenly in the same now, both walking backward, locked together the way Juno and Ash are. One key press turns a near miss into a chase.

That is worth showing before the move, not after. The lens makes the gap and its parity visible, so "invert to meet" and "invert to escape" become readable plays rather than accidents.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • The rule the design doc states in one line, that you reach another player only at a shared world turn, becomes something you can see instead of something you work out.
  • Four chips cost one row of chrome and answer the commonest question a split present raises: is anyone home in this turn?
  • Clicking a chip reuses the world-turn control that already exists, so the lens is navigation as well as readout.
  • The live and recorded distinction is drawn once, in a neutral treatment, and does not compete with the threat palette on page 10.
  • Parity makes inversion legible as a tool: the one action that does not spend a world turn is the one that decides who you can reach.

Cons

  • The forecast is a projection. It assumes nobody inverts, nobody is blocked and nobody is stuck, and all three happen. A confident wrong table is worse than none.
  • The chip row grows with the roster, and at six or eight players it stops being scannable.
  • It half-answers the horizon question by construction: chips go blank when an opponent walks past t12, which may leak more than it should or less than players want.
  • Chips both display and navigate. That makes them a control users press by accident while reading.
  • Another white ring on bodies that already carry colour, fill style, a personal index and sometimes a key mark.

What it answers

Serves the requirement for a timeline strip with each playhead and its direction, and puts the split-present rule from the design doc on screen: one combined board at a chosen world turn, with the lens saying whose present that turn is. It also makes the case that the strip is not optional decoration in a game with inversion, since with four playheads and one board there is otherwise no way to know whether a body you are planning against still has a player behind it.

It bears directly on the open question of whether players should see an opponent's horizon. The lens can only report a present it is allowed to see, so it goes blank on Juno and Ash from meta-turn 23, when they pass t12. That is a defensible default but it is a decision, not a fact, and it should be made deliberately.

It leaves open how the lens survives more than four players, and how a touch user reaches a chip without triggering a focus change. It also leaves the projection problem open: the forecast is only honest while it is labelled a projection, and nothing here proves players will read that label.

Cost

Chips and the same-turn ring: S, in ui.tsx and style.css only. The session view already carries every player's live body with its world turn and direction, so lighting a chip is a comparison against the focus turn, and clicking one sets the focus the world-turn control already sets.

Playhead rows: M, and they are the same component page 02 proposes. If that instrument lands, this lens is a second reading of its data rather than new drawing code.

The forecast: M, and mostly care rather than code. Projecting each playhead forward is a loop over direction; the work is labelling it a projection, stopping it at the horizon, and deciding whether to hide an opponent's row once their present passes t12. That last choice belongs in the design doc before it belongs in the UI.
