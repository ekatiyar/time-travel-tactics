Spacetime cube

Treat world time as a third spatial axis. Each world turn is a board layer; stack the layers vertically and draw every player's recorded bodies as a worldline threading through them. Inversion is a line that turns back down. A front is a plane cutting the stack. The current combined board is then just the top layer of this object, seen from above.

Variants

How you would play in it

  • Default view is the flat top layer, exactly today's board. The cube is a mode you enter by dragging the board's edge downward, or with Shift+scroll.
  • Focus layer follows the world-turn scrub. Scrubbing in cube mode slides the opaque layer up and down the stack rather than swapping the board's contents, which makes "where was everyone at t5" a spatial question.
  • Moves are still chosen on the focus layer. Ghost previews (page 08) extend one layer up or down depending on your direction, which makes "backward players move down" tangible.
  • Weapons mode: an erasure front and its restoration front become two planes with a shaded slab between them: the hole. A cycle is a periodic stack of slabs, which is the clearest picture of cycles I can think of.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • The rules say time is a dimension you can walk through. This is the only view that draws it as one.
  • Fronts, holes, horizons, and same-turn interaction all have a literal geometric meaning: plane, slab, ceiling, same layer.
  • Spectacular for onboarding, replay, and a share card even if play stays 2D.

Cons

  • Occlusion. A 16x9 board with 72 bodies produces line spaghetti; the mock already needs offsets and glass layers to stay readable. A 24x13 board with four players and 40 turns will not.
  • Precision. Choosing a move on a tilted layer is error-prone; keep input on the flat view.
  • Cost. Rendering is fine in SVG for this size, but hit-testing, hover, and animation in oblique space are a new subsystem. WebGL is the honest answer for boards above 20x10, which is a big dependency for a Preact prototype.
  • Screen height. The stack needs the full viewport; it competes with the timeline instrument (page 02), which shows much of the same information in 260px.

What it answers

Answers the combined-board requirement in a stronger form than a look-back slider. It gives cycles and holes a picture, which the strip cannot do as well. It does not answer touch inspection or small screens, and it should not be the primary play surface.

Cost
