Onboarding & rule hints

Nothing in the prototype teaches the game. A first-time player meets a board, four numbers, and a button called "Turn around in time". This direction adds three layers that cost nothing once learned: five coach marks on the first match, rule cards that appear the moment a rule first bites, and a permanent strip that reads this turn's six options as sentences.

  • 2The strip and the playheads. t0 to t12 is world time. Your playhead is at t8 pointing backward; the other dots are the other three presents.
  • 3The key and the grab tiles. One key on the center tile (8,4). Any of its four neighbours picks it up. The square on your body shows which side it hangs on.
  • 4Invert. Turning around costs an index but no world turn. Afterwards you walk back through history and may stand on your own earlier bodies.
  • 5Commit. Choices are simultaneous and private. You can change yours until the last player commits.
  • 6The trigger is written on the card, so the player learns that hints are caused rather than random, and trusts the dismiss button.
  • 7Each card carries the number from the rules, not a paraphrase: two indices a turn, four turns, indices 14 to 17. That is the same wording the inspector and the front tooltip use.
  • 8Every row names the tile and both world turns, so "t8 becomes t7" is stated rather than inferred from a direction arrow.
  • 9Invert is the row that needs the sentence most: it is the only action that does not spend a world turn, and the only one that changes what later rows will say.
  • 10A blocked row keeps its place and gains the reason. Disappearing options teach nothing; a greyed row with "Juno 17 holds (7,3)" teaches the tile rule in one reading.

Sixty-second interactive tutorial

A solo board with a scripted opponent, not a video. Every step is a real turn on a real engine, so the player commits eight times in a minute. Suggested board: 9 × 7, no walls, two colours.

  1. Move once. Only one tile is offered. You commit, the turn resolves, and a body is left behind on the tile you came from.0:00 – 0:08 · teaches: actions leave bodies
  2. Read your own number. The tutorial asks "what is your personal index now?" and accepts a tap on the body. It is 1, not t1.0:08 – 0:16 · teaches: index against world turn
  3. Walk to the key. Three free moves to a tile beside the center. The key attaches with the side square facing the center.0:16 – 0:28 · teaches: grab tiles, key side
  4. Get robbed. The scripted opponent steps to the tile beyond your key side and takes it. The log line and the front triangle both appear.0:28 – 0:36 · teaches: steals come from one specific tile
  5. Watch the front walk. Two turns of holding. The front advances two indices each turn and the strip shows the gap counting down.0:36 – 0:44 · teaches: two indices a turn, gap equals turns
  6. Turn around. Invert is the only offered action. Your playhead stops, your direction flips, and your body count grows by one.0:44 – 0:50 · teaches: inversion costs an index, not a turn
  7. Steal it back. Walking backward, you reach the opponent's recorded holder and rob a body that is not even live.0:50 – 0:56 · teaches: recorded bodies are real targets
  8. Win. One more backward step lands you at t0 beside your spawn holding the key. The win banner names the three conditions it just checked.0:56 – 1:04 · teaches: the win condition, in full

Trade-offs

Pros

  • The action strip is useful forever, not just on turn one, so the teaching layer is not dead weight after the first match.
  • Rule cards fire on a real event, which is when the rule is finally interesting. Nobody reads them up front.
  • Five coach marks is small enough to finish. The dot row shows that from the first card.
  • The tutorial is real turns on the real engine. No separate scripted mode to keep in sync with the rules.

Cons

  • Coach marks need stable anchors. Every layout change in ui.tsx can move a spotlight off its target.
  • Six sentences take more vertical room than a D-pad. On a phone the strip has to collapse to the picked action plus a count.
  • "Shown once" needs storage. Clearing site data restarts the lessons, and a shared device shows them to the wrong person.
  • The tutorial needs a scripted opponent, which is a second kind of session next to peer matches.

What it answers

Serves illegal moves and their reason on inspection from the opposite side to page 09: not on inspection but before you try, as a listed row with the reason attached. Restates the personal-index gap for an inbound front in a sentence a first-time player can act on. Uses the timeline strip with each playhead and direction as the subject of step 2 rather than assuming it is self-evident.

It also brushes should a player see their own known future: the action strip already tells you the world turn you will land on, which is the smallest possible version of showing the future, and it does not feel like cheating.

Leaves open: nothing here teaches erasure, restoration, cycles, or weapons, and the five-step frame will not stretch to cover them when they land.

Cost

S for the action strip, which is a map over view.actions into sentences, plus style.css. Nothing new from the engine: the offers already carry action, t, x, y, move, and reason. M for rule cards: each one needs a trigger predicate over the view plus a seen-flag in local storage. M to L for coach marks, mostly because anchoring to live DOM nodes wants refs and a small positioning helper, and the tutorial wants a scripted opponent inside transport.ts that never touches the network.
