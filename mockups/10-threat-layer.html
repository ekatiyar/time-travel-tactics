Threat & urgency layer

One optional layer over the board that answers two questions: which tiles an opponent can stand on at a world turn that matters to you, and how many meta-turns are left before a front lands. It is drawn in a palette no player owns, and it has an off / low / full control so a 16x9 board does not drown in it.

When things land

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Threat stops being a colour problem. Hot magenta, cool cyan and a grey hatch are three marks nobody on the roster can be confused with.
  • Certainty is carried by fill and pattern rather than hue, so the ordering survives colour blindness and a monochrome screenshot.
  • The off / low / full control makes the layer honest about its own cost. Low is three marks; off is today's board exactly.
  • Reachability over (tile, world turn) pairs teaches the rule that every action but inversion spends a world turn. The five-tile block at t8 is the rule made visible.
  • It surfaces the one fact that decides this match: the key cannot be walked home in time.

Cons

  • Hot magenta on a warm palette is loud, and on a small board almost every tile near the action ends up tinted.
  • Reach sets grow fast. Two turns of one opponent is eleven tiles; three opponents at three turns would tint most of the board and mean nothing.
  • Rings sit on top of bodies that already carry a colour, an outline style, a personal index and sometimes a key mark. That is five channels on one 32px token.
  • A forecast that assumes nobody inverts is wrong most turns, and a wrong countdown is worse than no countdown.
  • Three states is one more setting to explain, and a player who leaves it off never learns the rules the layer teaches.

What it answers

Takes a position on the open question of what visual treatment distinguishes urgent threats without overloading player colours: give urgency its own two-hue palette plus a hatch, keep it off the identity colours entirely, and let fill and pattern carry certainty so hue only ever means the role. Because it is a separate layer with an intensity control, the answer does not cost anything on boards where it is not wanted.

It satisfies the requirement to show the personal-index gap for an inbound front, on the board rather than in hover text, and sketches the cycle countdown the doc asks for with the same ring. It also proposes an answer to how fronts read when the front has no tile at the focused turn: a ring on the body it will take, plus a mark on a meta-turn rail.

It leaves open whether an opponent's front readout should count to their present or to their last visible body, since every number here is computed from what your horizon shows. It does not solve crowding on a 5x5 board, and it says nothing about touch: the rings are decoration, not hit targets, so page 09's inspector still has to carry inspection.

Cost

Palette, rings and steal tiles: S. Three CSS custom properties, a twelve-segment ring component, and the tile beyond a key side, which is one vector add on view.keys. The gap is already in view.fronts. The intensity control is a three-state button plus a localStorage key in ui.tsx.

Heat tiles: M. A breadth-first flood over (tile, world turn) pairs from each opponent's live playhead, bounded by a small step count, skipping walls and tiles already recorded to another player at that turn. The client has bodies, walls and playheads already, so this is a new computation rather than new state, but it belongs in a helper module and not in ui.tsx.

The rail: M, and it needs a small view addition. Marking which meta-turn each body drops the key needs the key index each holder sits at and each front's origin. ModeView in bootstrap.ts exposes holders and front positions but not those indices. It is the same addition page 06's custody ladder asks for, so the two should land together.
